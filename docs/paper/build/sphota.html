Can Meaning Have Universal Coordinates? Pre-Registered Negative Results, an Adapter Bottleneck, and What a Frozen Multilingual Code Can Still Do

The Sphota Authors

November 2026 (preprint draft, not yet submitted; cs.CL, cross-list cs.IR)

Abstract

We tried to build a universal coordinate system for meaning: a frozen, open, hierarchical code (e.g. JYZA-7M-X0-A8) that any text in any language receives, reachable from many embedding models through adapters, so that translations, paraphrases and different models agree on the same address. Before the decisive experiments we fixed a four-part pass/fail gate on held-out data (coverage, translation agreement, cross-model agreement, language collapse) and amended it to require at least 256 top-level cells. No configuration passes. The frozen release (4096 cells, multilingual-e5-small anchor) fails all four criteria; a GPU run at ~450k sentences with LaBSE and bge-m3 anchors removes language collapse and reaches 88% confident translation agreement at 256 cells, but cross-model agreement peaks at 50% (bar: 60%). A controlled adapter study then shows that much of this bottleneck is the linear adapter: a small residual MLP trained on the same pairs roughly doubles same-cell agreement with the anchor (five small models: 16.0% → 34.1% at 4096 cells, 32.6% → 56.1% at 256 cells; multilingual-e5-base: 30.9% → 70.5% at 256 cells), keeps cross-model retrieval at ~100% recall@10, and is still improving at 40k training pairs, while ridge is flat. An evaluation on six public domains (multilingual commands, banking support, technical Q&A, science, Indian-language reviews and code) shows what a frozen code can still do: its themes are a competitive zero-setup grouping (V-measure within 0.05–0.09 of per-dataset k-means on science, technical and review text, and 0.25–0.29 above TF-IDF there), descriptions and code share a region 77–96% of the time, and codes stay stable across runs. It also shows the limits: short informal commands share a region across languages only 36–56% of the time, far below FLORES, and narrow domains (banking intents) need per-dataset refinement. Training the space on short parallel commands lifts their agreement from 42% to 72% at 256 cells but causes language collapse elsewhere. A six-round pre-registered study of per-file refinement, each round judged once on fresh data, ends short of its bar (final round: 18 of 26 datasets within 0.05 of an oracle-k reference) yet beats the frozen themes on 38 of 42 fresh sets and groups translations together about twice as often. We measure 19 Indian languages (13 work well; Santali and Manipuri fail, Santali confidently wrong), report two practical lessons on labels, and release the code, artifacts, gate, negative results and every script.

1. Introduction

Embedding vectors are a good computational representation of meaning and a poor data format: a vector from model A means nothing to model B, it cannot be read or grouped in SQL, and every model upgrade forces re-embedding the corpus. A natural ambition is a shared, stable address for meaning, like latitude and longitude: readable, hierarchical, frozen, and the same whichever language or model produced the text.

This paper asks whether that ambition is achievable with current multilingual encoders and simple, transparent machinery, and, where it is not, what remains useful. Our answer has four parts:

  • Negative (pre-registered). A fine-grained universal code (hundreds to thousands of top-level cells, stable across languages and models) is not supported, on CPU-scale or GPU-scale data, with three anchors.
  • Diagnostic. The binding constraint at fine resolution is agreement between models, and a large part of it is the adapter, not the models: nonlinear adapters roughly double it.
  • Positive. A coarse shared partition, the frozen first code block as a grouping key, and the continuous canonical space as a migration hub are useful, including on real data far from the fitting corpus.
  • Sobering. Real, short, informal text is much harder than the parallel benchmark used to build the system; we quantify how much.

We think the negative result and the gap between benchmark and real data are worth publishing on their own. “Semantic IDs” and shared embedding spaces are active topics, and claims of a universal meaning code are easy to make on in-sample numbers; ours looked good in-sample too (81% of training sentences get a confident top-level code versus 26% held out).

The project name comes from Bhartrhari’s sphota (5th c. CE): meaning that bursts forth whole and stays the same across its utterances. Our results suggest it stays the same only at a coarse resolution, for now.

2. Related work

Semantic IDs. TIGER (Rajput et al., 2023) quantizes item embeddings with an RQ-VAE into codeword tuples for generative retrieval; DSI (Tay et al., 2022) uses hierarchical k-means document IDs; Singh et al. (2024) study Semantic IDs for ranking. These codes are private to one system and refit per catalogue, so they never face the cross-language and cross-model stability test that a universal code must pass. Codebook collapse in RQ-VAE is mitigated by EMA updates and dead-code revival (Zeghidour et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2022).

Embedding translation. vec2vec (Jha et al., 2025) translates between embedding spaces without paired data, in support of the Platonic representation hypothesis (Huh et al., 2024). Supervised linear maps between embedding spaces are a long-standing baseline (e.g. cross-lingual word embedding mapping). Our results are compatible with the hypothesis at the level of continuous geometry (migration works) while showing that discrete agreement is far weaker, and that the gap between linear and nonlinear maps matters at fine resolution.

Language-agnostic spaces. LASER, LaBSE (Feng et al., 2022) and SONAR (Duquenne et al., 2023) embed many languages jointly; Large Concept Models (2024) operate in SONAR space. We quantify how much of their cross-lingual alignment survives discretization.

Topic models and clustering benchmarks. BERTopic (Grootendorst, 2022) clusters embeddings and labels clusters with c-TF-IDF, refitting per corpus. MTEB (Muennighoff et al., 2023) evaluates embedding clustering with V-measure on per-dataset k-means. Our positive result is close to a frozen, multilingual, shared BERTopic-style partition; we report it against MTEB-style per-dataset clustering so the cost of freezing is explicit.

3. Method

3.1 Anchor encoder. intfloat/multilingual-e5-small (MIT, 384-d, ~100 languages), input "query: " + text, mean pooling, L2 normalization. Chosen in a bake-off against bge-m3, LaBSE and paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM for speed, size and an exact ONNX export (max abs diff 7·10⁻⁸ versus PyTorch); it runs on a laptop CPU and in the browser. §5 revisits the choice.

3.2 Canonical space (meaning-LDA). With unit vectors x, y = (x − μ) W: PCA to 384 directions, then the generalized eigenvectors of (S_total, S_within + reg), where S_within is the scatter of translations of the same sentence (FLORES-200 dev, 800 meanings × 12 languages; 7.2k Tatoeba pairs), shrunk toward isotropy (reg = 0.05). The top 256 directions are scaled so within-meaning noise has unit variance. PCA alone gives 34.1% cross-lingual L1 agreement versus 46.1% with meaning-LDA (4096 cells).

3.3 Quantizer and code. Residual k-means with 4 levels (4096·256·256·256) on 77.6k sentences (Wikipedia sample in 12 languages + FLORES dev). Codes are Crockford base32 (widths 4/2/2/2) after a published affine permutation, so 85% of single-symbol typos are rejected without a checksum. A separate layer of 32 coarse regions (k-means on the canonical space) is stored alongside, outside the frozen artifact.

3.4 Confidence. At each level, a softmax over the 16 nearest centroids’ squared distances, with a temperature fitted by maximum likelihood to predict the held-out translation’s symbol; the product over levels is the prefix confidence.

3.5 Adapters. Other models enter the canonical space through maps fitted on 40k paired texts (registry: all-MiniLM-L6-v2, bge-small-en-v1.5, gte-small, paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2, LaBSE). The frozen release uses ridge regression on L2-normalized model vectors. Set v1.1 adds a residual network on the input, y = xA + b + gelu(xW₁ + c₁)W₂ + c₂ (one 1024-unit hidden layer, tanh-approximated GELU, MSE loss, AdamW, 40 epochs), shipped as a NumPy-only file (float16 hidden weights) and certified with the NumPy forward pass. An unsupervised vec2vec-style adapter failed at our CPU budget (top-1 retrieval 0% versus 96.5% for ridge on the same texts).

4. The decision gate

Owner review of the first release concluded that in-sample numbers did not support a “GPS for meaning” claim. We therefore fixed the following gate before any v2 run. All criteria use held-out FLORES-200 devtest sentences in 12 languages, never used to fit the space, codebooks, calibration, labels or adapters.

# Criterion Definition Bar
1 Coverage share of held-out sentences (all 12 languages) with L1 confidence ≥ 0.7 ≥ 60%
2 Translation agreement among en↔︎x pairs whose English code has L1 confidence ≥ 0.7, share with the same L1 code (mean over 11 languages) ≥ 80%
3 Cross-model agreement same English text, certified adapter vs anchor, same L1 code (mean over adapters) ≥ 60%
4 Language collapse per language, share of its sentences in L1 cells whose training members are ≥ 80% that language (English excluded) ≤ 5% for every language

Amendment. A tiny-scale dry run “passed” all four criteria with 16 top-level cells. A gate that can be passed by making the partition coarse says nothing about coordinates, so the gate must be met with ≥ 256 top-level cells, on all 12 languages, with the cell count declared before the run.

8. Per-file refinement: six pre-registered rounds

Frozen themes are stable but generic; one file is often narrow (all banking support) or mixed (the same complaint in four languages). We therefore studied per-file refinement: groups fitted to one file, each tied to a stable theme code, with every message’s own code unchanged. Before building anything we fixed four targets (D-033): R1 grouping quality within 0.05 V-measure of k-means that is told the true number of labels (or uses our number of groups, whichever is better), on every dataset; R2 determinism (row order cannot change the groups; ARI ≥ 0.95); R3 in files mixing two languages, translations share a refined group at least as often as they share a fixed theme; R4 ≤ 2 s for 3,000 messages.

Protocol. Each dataset was split once into dev (30%) and test (70%). Candidates were chosen on dev only, and each round evaluated only the dev winner, once, on data no method had touched: round 1 on the test splits, rounds 2–6 on new confirmation sets (more MASSIVE languages, the MASSIVE validation split, new Stack Exchange and arXiv splits, MasakhaNEWS train, FLORES devtest). Every amendment was committed before the run it affected, and a pipeline smoke test that accidentally reported test numbers was disclosed (D-034). 95% bootstrap intervals accompany each headline number in the result files.

Each round fixed the failure the previous one exposed: an ordering bug (R2), a silhouette rule that picked too few groups (R1), script-based language correction that cannot tell English from Italian (R3; replaced by a 51-language identifier trained on MASSIVE train, 94% accurate), and a dev set that lacked same-script pairs and so could not see that failure (a methodological lesson: the dev set must contain the failure mode being fixed). The final method does not meet the bar (round 6: R1 18/26, R3 11/12). The residual R1 failure is structural: the reference may use the true number of labels, and no label-free rule fits both fine label sets (59–77 intents) and coarse, well-separated ones (12–17 Stack Exchange sites, 7 news topics).

Against what users had before (the fixed themes), the refined groups win on 38 of 42 fresh sets (mean V-measure gain +0.05 to +0.10; Banking77 0.42 → 0.69–0.74), keep translations together about twice as often, run in 0.1 s, and lose only on four languages the anchor barely knows (−0.01 to −0.02). We ship them as the default for files of ≥ 200 messages, in the library and in the browser (a TypeScript port that agrees exactly with the Python implementation on the same vectors).

10. Why fine-grained universal codes fail

Three effects compound. (1) Boundary mass: equivalent texts are small clouds, not points; the fraction of clouds cut by some cell boundary rises with the number of cells. (2) Adapter error: each adapter adds its own displacement, which at fine granularity exceeds the cell radius. §6 shows this term is partly reducible: nonlinear adapters halve the gap at 256 cells. (3) Residual language signal: at fine granularity k-means can profitably spend cells on language. Confidence and alternates make (1) visible but do not remove it. A code can be calibrated and honest about its uncertainty and still not be universal.

11. What works, and practical lessons

Coarse regions and themes. A frozen first code block is a stable grouping key for analytics (SQL GROUP BY, dashboards, trends over time), routing and cache keys, with finer levels as optional detail carrying explicit confidence.

Migration through stored canonical vectors. Documents indexed with model X through its adapter and queried with the anchor reach recall@10 of 96.5–99.1% (ridge) and ~100% (v1.1) on FLORES, versus 100% for re-embedding; codes alone give 10–28%. Storing the 256-d canonical vector lets an organisation change models without re-embedding, with N adapters instead of N² pairwise maps.

Lesson 1: labels from a general corpus mislead out of domain. Cell names generated from Wikipedia members describe everyday text badly (“my order hasn’t arrived” → Kali Yuga Tenets and Authors; “Messi scored twice in the final” → Golf Championships, at L1 confidence 0.79). Hiding labels below a confidence threshold left 7 of 48 support messages labelled, and those 7 were still wrong. Naming each group by its most confident member from the user’s own data works better.

Lesson 2: machine-translated labels need a semantic check. Script and length filters miss fluent nonsense (Bengali Automotive Innovations → “Unique Violence”). A ranking test (the translated name or its back-translation must point to its own label among all labels) removed 30 of ~550 names across 11 languages.

Lesson 3: releasing open ML artifacts safely. Pin every downloaded model to a hub commit and verify file hashes (a silently updated model changes “frozen” codes); never load pickles or remote model code; serve browser runtimes from the site itself (our in-browser encoder’s library fetched a development build of its runtime from a CDN until a Content Security Policy caught it); build wheels in an isolated job with hash-pinned tools and refuse any wheel that is not byte-identical to the tracked source (the 2026 compromises of a widely used Python package shipped a .pth startup hook); publish with provenance attestations and no stored tokens.

12. Limitations

Most experiments are CPU-scale (≤ 80k fitting sentences, 12 languages); one GPU run reached ~450k sentences. We did not combine nonlinear adapters with a strong anchor at GPU scale. Region and cell labels are machine-generated and not natively reviewed. The six domains are English-heavy outside MASSIVE and IndicReviews; IndicReviews is machine- or human-translated parallel text whose translation process we did not audit. Per-dataset k-means is a reference, not a ceiling; stronger clustering (e.g. HDBSCAN) was not tried. FLORES dev was used to fit the space, so no FLORES-derived dataset (e.g. SIB-200) appears in §7. The refinement study spent six evaluation rounds on related benchmark families; although every round used items no method had seen, the method was adapted between rounds, so later rounds are less independent than the first. The language identifier is trained on MASSIVE train, a sibling of the MASSIVE test and validation sets used for evaluation.

13. Conclusion

Can meaning have universal coordinates? At fine resolution, with today’s small multilingual encoders, our pre-registered answer is no: no build passes a fixed, held-out gate at ≥ 256 cells. But the failure is not a wall: a large part of it is the linear adapter, and nonlinear adapters close half the gap at the gate’s granularity with no new data. At coarse resolution the answer is a qualified yes, with an important caveat measured here for the first time on real text: short informal messages agree across languages far less often than parallel benchmarks suggest. Two levers we measured move these numbers, each with a price: teaching the space short parallel text (a 30-point gain for short commands, paid for by language collapse elsewhere at fine granularity), and fitting groups per file (better than frozen themes on 38 of 42 fresh sets, but short of an oracle that knows how many categories the user has in mind). We recommend that work on semantic IDs and shared meaning spaces report held-out, cross-language and cross-model agreement as a function of granularity, together with language collapse, evaluate on real text outside the fitting distribution, and fix the bar before running.
